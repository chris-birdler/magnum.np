Powder Core Loss Equation

One equation that shows how each material and process parameter moves the power loss. It gives the direction and the strength of each trend. It does not give exact loss values.

The equation

\[ P \;=\; \underbrace{\Big[\,1 + p_\mathrm{c}\,\big(\kappa_0 + \kappa_a\,a_\mathrm{c}\big)\Big]}_{\text{contacts}} \;\Bigg[\;\underbrace{c_\mathrm{m}\, f\, K_\mathrm{eff}\, \underbrace{\big(1 + \gamma_\mathrm{h}\,h\big)}_{\text{homogeneity}} \underbrace{\big(1 + \gamma_\mathrm{s}\,\varphi_\mathrm{s}\big)}_{\text{surface}} \left(\frac{B_\mathrm{peak}}{J_\mathrm{s}}\right)^{\!\beta} \underbrace{\left[1 + \left(\frac{f}{f_\mathrm{r}}\right)^{\!\alpha - 1}\right]}_{\text{static + dynamic}}}_{\text{magnetic}} \;+\;\underbrace{\frac{\pi^{2}\,d^{2}\,f^{2}\,B_\mathrm{peak}^{2}}{20\,\rho}}_{\text{eddy current}}\;\Bigg] \]
Resonance frequency (Snoek), with the permeability from \(K_\mathrm{eff}\)
\[ f_\mathrm{r} \;=\; \frac{\gamma\, J_\mathrm{s}}{3\pi\,\big(\mu_\mathrm{r} - 1\big)}, \qquad \mu_\mathrm{r} - 1 \;=\; \frac{J_\mathrm{s}^{2}}{3\,\mu_0\, K_\mathrm{eff}} \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad f_\mathrm{r} \;=\; \frac{\gamma\,\mu_0\, K_\mathrm{eff}}{\pi\, J_\mathrm{s}} \]

Far below \(f_\mathrm{r}\) the magnetic part is the static hysteresis (a fixed loss per cycle). Towards \(f_\mathrm{r}\) a dynamic part adds. Its exponent \(\alpha\) of 1.5 is a shape borrowed from the excess loss of wall motion [10, 12]; a linear damping relaxation gives at most 2. The permeability \(\mu_\mathrm{r}\) of the particle material is not a separate input: in the same rotation model, it follows from \(K_\mathrm{eff}\) and \(J_\mathrm{s}\). It is not the permeability of the core, which is 26 to 60 for typical powder cores [9]. A small effective anisotropy gives a high permeability and a low resonance frequency; at the base values (stress-free grains) \(\mu_\mathrm{r}\) is about 10⁵ and \(f_\mathrm{r}\) about 225 kHz [11, 13].

Volume fraction of the surface layer (sphere of diameter \(d\), layer thickness \(\delta_\mathrm{s}\))
\[ \varphi_\mathrm{s} \;=\; 1 - \left(1 - \frac{2\,\delta_\mathrm{s}}{d}\right)^{\!3} \;\approx\; \delta_\mathrm{s}\,\frac{S}{V} \;=\; \frac{6\,\delta_\mathrm{s}}{d} \]

For a thin layer, \(\varphi_\mathrm{s}\) is the layer thickness times the surface-to-volume ratio \(S/V\) of the particle. When the particle diameter comes down to twice the layer thickness, the whole particle is surface layer: \(\varphi_\mathrm{s}\) is then 1 and the loss stops rising.

Minimum of \(P\) over the particle diameter (from the equation; exponent \(\beta\) of 2, thin layer)
\[ d_\mathrm{opt} \;\approx\; \left(\frac{60\,\gamma_\mathrm{s}\,\delta_\mathrm{s}\, c_\mathrm{m}\, K_\mathrm{eff}\, \big(1 + \gamma_\mathrm{h} h\big)\, \rho}{\pi^{2}\, J_\mathrm{s}^{2}\, f} \left[1 + \left(\frac{f}{f_\mathrm{r}}\right)^{\!\alpha - 1}\right]\right)^{\!1/3} \]

\(d_\mathrm{opt}\) is not an extra input: it follows from the surface factor and the eddy current term, which change in opposite directions with \(d\). Finer powders are better only down to \(d_\mathrm{opt}\).

Contact factor

Particles that touch increase both parts: currents cross the contacts, and the flux concentrates in the contact necks. The increase is linear in the contact probability; the contact area makes this slope steeper, also linearly.

Magnetic part with homogeneity and surface

Static hysteresis plus a dynamic part that grows towards the resonance frequency. The effective anisotropy sets the loss per cycle; spatial fluctuations of the anisotropy and the disturbed surface layer of small particles increase it. The drive level sets how far the material goes towards saturation.

Eddy current part (classical sphere)

Currents inside each particle. They increase with the particle size and the frequency, and decrease with the resistivity.

\(K_\mathrm{eff}\) of a nanocrystalline material (Herzer averaging, outside the equation)

\[ K_\mathrm{eff} \;=\; \frac{K_1^{4}\,D^{6}}{A^{3}} \;=\; K_1\left(\frac{D}{L_0}\right)^{\!6}, \qquad L_0 \;=\; \sqrt{\frac{A}{K_1}} \]

In a nanocrystalline material, the exchange averages the crystal anisotropy \(K_1\) of the grains over the length \(L_0\). The grain size \(D\) and the exchange constant \(A\) set the remaining effective anisotropy. This applies while \(D\) is smaller than \(L_0\); for larger grains the coercivity falls again, in inverse proportion to \(D\) [1]. With \(K_1\) of 8 kJ/m³, \(A\) of 10 pJ/m and \(D\) of 10 nm, the result is about 4 J/m³ (the base value, stress-free grains); \(L_0\) is about 35 nm. A magnetoelastic anisotropy from magnetostriction and stress adds to \(K_\mathrm{eff}\).

{{CARDS}}

Limits

Trends, not values

The constants \(c_\mathrm{m}\), \(\alpha\), \(\gamma_\mathrm{h}\), \(\gamma_\mathrm{s}\), \(\kappa_0\) and \(\kappa_a\) and the layer thickness \(\delta_\mathrm{s}\) are illustrative. The equation shows the direction and the power of each trend. The split between the magnetic and the eddy current part at the base point follows from the illustrative \(c_\mathrm{m}\). A real core needs a fit of the constants to measurements.

Loss per volume of particle material

\(P\) is the loss per volume of the particle material at the core flux density \(B_\mathrm{peak}\), as on datasheets. For the loss per core volume, multiply by the packing fraction. In our micromagnetic model (old setup, packing fraction 0.55 to 0.69, at a fixed core flux density) the packing fraction had no clear effect on the loss per volume of particle material. Inside the particles the flux density is higher than in the core.

Homogeneity term

Spatial fluctuations of the anisotropy (stress, composition, defects) hold the magnetization back; in the pinning theories of domain walls the coercive field increases with their amplitude. The linear form is a set rule for this showroom.

Particle size

Measured powder cores show the same trade-off: coarser powders have a lower coercive field and less hysteresis loss, finer powders less eddy current loss; a median size of 124 µm was near the minimum of the loss at 50 to 300 Hz [6]; other studies report the same trade-off [5, 7] (not checked for this page). The mixture of a particle core and a surface layer with its own loss is a set rule. Our micromagnetic model has no disturbed surface layer and gives no evidence for this term.

Eddy currents in a real core

The eddy current term is the classical loss of a single sphere [2]. With imperfect insulation, a real core also has a macroscopic eddy current loss at the scale of the core cross-section; its correction coefficient depends on the material [4]. In FeSi powder cores, an additional eddy current loss that depends on the grain size was measured [2]. For flake-shaped particles, the flake thickness replaces the diameter, with the factor 6 in place of 20.

Contact term

Contacts act on both parts. Conducting contacts let eddy currents flow across particle boundaries, so the current loops become larger. The flux concentrates in the contact necks, which increases the magnetic and the eddy current part. The linear form in the contact probability and in the contact area is a set rule for this showroom. Eddy currents through contacts at the scale of the core cross-section grow with the core size [4]; a factor on the particle terms cannot show this.

Exponents

The dynamic part grows with the exponent \(\alpha\). The value 1.5 is a shape borrowed from the excess loss of wall motion [10, 12], which is an eddy current loss with its own dependence on \(\rho\) and on the flux density; a linear damping relaxation gives at most 2. The flux density exponent \(\beta\) is usually 2 to 3 [8], 1.6 for a powder core datasheet [9] and 3 in the Rayleigh range at low fields. Our micromagnetic model (particles of about 1 µm, no eddy currents, \(K_\mathrm{eff}\) 1 to 6 kJ/m³, coarse mesh) gives \(\beta\) of 1.9 to 2.3 for the total rate-dependent loss at 30 MHz (4 design points). There, the loss depends on the rate at all tested flux densities: between 15 and 60 MHz its frequency exponent is about 1 to 4 (one design point, old setup, 3 seeds). The plots use 1.5 for \(\alpha\) and 2 for \(\beta\).

Resonance and permeability

\(f_\mathrm{r}\) marks the frequency where the dynamic part becomes as large as the static part; the equation has no resonance peak and no fall above \(f_\mathrm{r}\). The permeability is the one of the particle material, from the same rotation model as the Snoek relation. In a powder core, the resonance of the composite moves to higher frequencies [13]; the resonance of the particle material does not move, and we take it to set the loss at a given flux density in the particles (our inference). The \(f_\mathrm{r}\) formula equals the anisotropy-field frequency of our record. With the \(K_\mathrm{eff}\) of our micromagnetic model (1 to 6 kJ/m³) it gives 47 to 290 MHz, the same order as the onset of the rate dependence there (42 to 85 MHz at 9 mT, one design point). Whether \(f_\mathrm{r}\) sets this onset is open.

Mechanism

The Herzer model describes the averaging of the anisotropy in exchange-coupled grains. In our micromagnetic powder model, moving vortex cores are the main regions of the loss outside the drive frequency; the mesh does not resolve them. Our data do not yet confirm the single-factor trends of this page; their directions come from the literature.

Not in the equation

DC bias: a direct current in the winding moves the operating point towards saturation and decreases the permeability; the Steinmetz form ignores it [10]. Temperature: it acts through \(K_\mathrm{eff}\), \(J_\mathrm{s}\) and \(\rho\). Waveform: the equation applies to a sinusoidal flux; other waveforms need a generalized Steinmetz form [10].
